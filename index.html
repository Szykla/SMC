A record says what should have happened. It cannot say which product is in front of you.

Serial numbers, codes and databases describe a product, and all of them can be copied onto another one. MediCoinSwiss is developing TrustLayer®, which is designed to tie the record to the physical unit itself.

The Coin in the name stands for a minted mark of authenticity. Medicoinswiss AG is a Swiss company limited by shares and issues no tokens and no cryptocurrency.

On the product
A transponder (NFC or RFID), built into the device or sealed into its pack.
In the record
The transponder’s identity, bound to the product data by a cryptographic signature.
At the scan
A phone or a reader is meant to check both together and log the result.

Where it matters.

UDI: a code is not proof
The EU Medical Device Regulation and the Swiss Medical Devices Ordinance require unique device identification (UDI) and traceability. A code alone does not show that it sits on the right unit.
Digital Product Passport
The EU Ecodesign Regulation is introducing the Digital Product Passport product group by product group. Where a passport is kept per item, its entries need something physical to refer to.
At the point of use
Where a product is finally used, in a clinic or in home care, it should be possible to check that it is the unit the record describes.

Rendering of the demonstration pack, which is not a medical device. Both packs use this one picture: to the eye there is no difference.

Both packs carry the same record with a valid signature, because the copier copied that too. The difference is the chip. It holds a key that is designed never to leave it, so a copy cannot answer a question it has not seen before. This copy can only repeat an answer it once overheard.

Chip authentication of this kind is established technology. What TrustLayer® is designed to add is the binding of the chip to the product and its signed record. That binding is the subject of the patent application and is not shown here.

What just happened, exactly
  1. The manufacturer’s key signed the record once, when this page was built (ECDSA, curve P-256, SHA-256). The record contains the chip’s public key.
  2. At each scan your browser draws 16 random bytes and puts them to the chip as a question (a challenge). The chip signs the question together with the digest of the record.
  3. Your browser then verifies two signatures through its Web Crypto interface: the manufacturer’s over the record, and the chip’s over the question. Nothing is sent to a server.
  4. The copy sends an answer recorded from the original at an earlier scan. The signature is genuine, but it belongs to another question, and your browser shows which one.
  5. Not covered here: a relay of the question to a genuine chip somewhere else, and a genuine chip moved onto another pack.
  6. The pauses between the steps are there so that you can read along. The checks themselves take a few milliseconds.

Only the chip is simulated. Its key ships with this page so that your browser can play its part; the key of a real chip is designed never to leave it. All keys were made for this demonstration. The method shown, challenge and response, is a common one for authenticating a transponder.
